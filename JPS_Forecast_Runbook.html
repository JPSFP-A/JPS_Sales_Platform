Before you start

Three preconditions. Skipping any of them produces a forecast that looks complete and is wrong.

Check the session is live A stale login does not log you out. It returns zero rows with no error, because row-level security filters everything and reports success. The page then renders an all-zero forecast that is indistinguishable from real data. The app now shows a red banner when it detects this. If you see it, sign in again and reload; do not trust anything on screen until you have.
Wait for commercial detail RT40, RT50 and RT70 load lazily. Until they arrive they read zero, not blank, so every rate-class total, every loss rate and every committed budget built in that window is understated by roughly a third. The Commit and Exec Summary buttons refuse to run until it lands. Nothing else does, so give it a moment.
Check nobody else is mid-edit Assumptions are shared. Two people editing the same rate class, or a second Claude session writing SQL, will silently overwrite each other. See Where everything lives for the ownership split.

The cycle

01

Load the month's actuals

Admin → Data Upload

The billing extract is the ground truth everything else is built on. The forecast base is a trailing three-month average, so a bad upload moves every future month, not just the one you loaded.

  1. Choose Overwrite month for a normal monthly load: it clears that month first, so a re-run after a correction cannot double-count.
  2. Choose Append / upsert only when adding rows to a month that is already correct.
  3. Pick the Excel file. Columns are detected automatically; rows with no year or month are dropped and counted in the console.
  4. Confirm the row count looks like a month, not a fragment.
02

Clean what the upload brought in

Data Quality

Two jobs, both immediately after the upload while the data is fresh in mind.

  1. Rebuild net billers. One button. Re-derives every month from the <Zero consumption bucket and overwrites, so running it twice is harmless.
  2. Review customer names. Work the Needs review queue. Each group was merged by the matcher and no person has confirmed it.
A shared name is a suggestion, not a fact Grace Kennedy Remittance and Grace Kennedy Foods normalise to the same key and are different companies. Confirming that merge overstates one customer and makes the other disappear. When they are genuinely different, press Not the same. That decision is recorded and the matcher will not re-merge them.

Merging changes how customers are grouped for reporting. It moves no kWh: account totals, rate-class volumes and the forecast are untouched.

03

Set the driver assumptions

Macro Assumptions

Macro, weather, seasonality, migration, normalisation and per-industry rates, by month. Every non-zero change demands a reason code and a justification before it saves, and lands in the Adjustment Log.

  1. Set the rate in the month cell. The Implied FY column shows what it compounds to over twelve months.
  2. Read the before → after line in the reason dialog. That is the annual effect of the change you are about to make.
  3. Record why. The justification is the only thing that survives to the next person.
A rate is not an outcome The Implied FY figure is what this driver alone contributes. It is not the volume growth that comes out of the engine, because seasonality, weather, solar and normalisation all apply on top, against a rolling base. A driver set to a true 3.5%/yr rate delivered 1.37% RT10 volume growth. Decide which of the two you are setting, and record it in the basis field: rate if you are setting the rate and taking whatever falls out, volume_target if you back-solved to hit a number.

A rate-class row overrides the segment row: macro / RT10 beats macro / residential for RT10 accounts.

04

Enter account-level moves

Driver Forecast

Defections, expansions, shutdowns and renewals for named accounts, mostly entered by KAMs against their own customers.

  1. Find the account and enter the percentage move for the months it applies to.
  2. Pick the reason code that matches: confirmed defection, possible defection, operational disruption, expansion, renewal.
  3. Check the effect on the account's own line before leaving the row.
One month does not make a permanent change The base is a rolling three-month average, so a single month's adjustment washes out almost entirely by the next quarter. A sustained change needs three consecutive months at the level you want. A plant shutdown at −80% for one month lands as roughly −35% on the year; the same figure across three months holds at −84%.
05

Add new customers

Driver Forecast → New Customer

Load that does not exist yet and therefore has no history for the engine to grow. Entered as expected kWh from a start month.

  1. Enter the account, expected monthly kWh and the month it energises.
  2. Put the start month in the year the load actually begins: a customer starting in January belongs in that year, not carried in the prior one.
06

Add overlays for what the engine cannot express

Forecast Overlays

Rate-class and month adjustments in kWh, applied after the account walk. Use one when a movement belongs to no single account, such as a storm true-up, a reconciliation to a submitted total.

  1. Give it a label. That label is how the movement appears in the budget and the exec summary.
  2. Enter the months. The running total updates as you type.
  3. Write the rationale. It is mandatory, because an overlay exists precisely where the engine's own logic does not reach.
Prefer an account or a driver An overlay is untraceable by design. It belongs to nothing. If the movement can be expressed as an account adjustment or a driver rate, do it there instead, where it is visible in the walk.
07

Phase the losses

Macro Assumptions → Monthly Loss Phasing

The rolling 12-month rate is what gets reported. The losses team phases against the monthly rate underneath it. This works the second out of the first.

  1. Enter the twelve rolling targets in the grid above, December last, because December's window is the calendar year, so it is the annual rate.
  2. Pick the year range and press Solve. Both methods run; the comparison table shows monthly range, annual close and worst rolling miss for each.
  3. Read the exact solve first, as a test. If every month lands inside 21–31%, the targets are consistent with the sales phasing. If one does not, they are not, and no amount of solving will fix that.
  4. Switch to Smoothed for the series to hand the losses team. It holds the annual close exactly and gives up a fraction of a point on the intermediate rolling months.
  5. Save. The method is recorded with the values, because the numbers alone do not reveal which produced them.
An impossible month is a verdict on the targets Inverting near-flat rolling targets against seasonal sales forces the monthly losses to swing to compensate, worst at January where the window drops a December and picks up a January. The FY2027–28 targets originally produced a 0.57% January, and the distortion compounded year on year: January read 15.89% in the first year and 0.57% in the second, because each year's solve stands on the prior year's series. A third year would have been worse again. The arithmetic was exact throughout; the targets were wrong. They have since been rebuilt and now invert cleanly at 22.7–30.8%.
Three methods, and they are not interchangeable Exact and smoothed both take the rolling targets as given and differ only in how faithfully they reproduce them. Derived does not take them as given at all. The monthly path is built forwards from observed seasonality with the level solved to close the year, and the rolling series is computed from that. Which one produced a series is recorded in method.
08

Review before you commit

Driver Forecast · 3-Year Comparison

Everything above is now in the numbers. This is where you find out whether it is right.

  1. Walk the Driver Forecast tab by rate class. Each month decomposes into base and every driver, so an odd total can be traced to the term that caused it.
  2. Check for clamped months. A driver sum below −100% is floored so a positive base cannot go negative; a clamped month means the assumptions stacked past total elimination.
  3. Open 3-Year Comparison and read the year-on-year movements. Anything you cannot explain in a sentence is not ready to commit.
  4. Generate the Exec Summary from the years selected there, and it re-derives from the live engine, so it moves when assumptions move.
  5. Build the pack in Report Builder.
09

Commit and publish

3-Year Comparison → Commit as Budget

Freezes the selected years at account and cohort grain. Database triggers make a locked version immutable. This is the point of no return.

  1. Confirm commercial detail has loaded. The button blocks if it has not.
  2. Commit. The version is written with its full driver decomposition, overlays as their own rows, and an assumption snapshot.
  3. Push to the Finance Hub when the budget is agreed.
Verify the write Check the version appears with a row count before you tell anyone it is committed. This function silently did nothing for a period, see Known traps.

How the engine works

Four mechanics explain most surprising results.

// every forecast month, per account or cohort total = base × (1 + Σ drivers ⁄ 100) + manual_kwh base = trailing 3-month rolling average drivers = macro + weather + seasonality + solar + normalisation + migration + operational Σ is floored at −100%

The base rolls. Each forecast month feeds the next month's base, so effects compound and single-month adjustments decay. This is why a permanent change needs three consecutive months.

Years cascade. FY2027 needs October–December 2026, so computing a future year computes every year before it. Change a 2026 assumption and 2028 moves.

Carry-in preserves level shifts. The prior year's October–December tail carries forward, deseasonalised for the residential classes. That tail is what keeps a confirmed mid-year defection gone in the following year rather than averaging it back to life.

Rate class beats segment. A driver keyed to a rate class overrides the segment-level row for accounts in that class.

Known traps

Each of these has already produced a wrong number in this forecast. They are listed because they are not obvious, not because they are hypothetical.

Reading a monthly rate as an annual one

Driver rates apply monthly against a rolling base and compound. 0.676%/month is 8.42%/year, not 0.68%.

What happenedAn RT10 assumption was read as roughly 8% growth and flagged as a 30 GWh error. It was not. The value had been solved to deliver 2.8% volume growth. The correction to the correction cost more time than the original. The Implied FY column exists because of this.

Averaging two regimes into one trend

Before concluding a decline is structural, split the window. A damaged period and a recovered period averaged together produce a trend that describes neither.

What happenedSolar export per net biller looked 21.3% down year on year and was nearly written into the report as erosion. Split by period it was −50.6% in January–February and −4.7% by May–July: hurricane damage, already recovered. Because the base is the trailing three months, the recovery was already in the forecast. Booking it again would have double-counted.

Confusing a level shift with a recurring rate

A customer that leaves is a permanent step down. A growth rate is a slope. Entering one as the other resurrects load that is gone or compounds a one-off forever.

What happenedA carry-in change averaged a confirmed 100% self-generating industrial customer back into the following year, restoring load that no longer existed. Caught only because someone asked why the impact was missing.

Trusting a screen that has silently signed out

An expired session returns zero rows and no error. Every total reads zero, which looks like a real number.

What happenedA forecast rendered entirely from zeros. A blocking banner now appears when this is detected, and idle sign-out is four hours, but idle sign-out clears the shared platform cookie, so an idle tab on any platform app signs you out of all of them.

Two people writing the same assumption

Nothing locks an assumption while you edit it. The last write wins, silently.

What happenedTwo sessions re-solved the same accounts against each other for a full day, each reacting to changes the other had made without knowing. Resolved by splitting ownership explicitly, see below.

Reading a round trip as confirmation

If a monthly series was derived, built forwards from observed seasonality, with the rolling targets computed from it, then inverting those targets returns the same monthly path. Exact match, in band, both years agreeing, no warnings. It confirms nothing; it is the round trip closing.

This is more dangerous than a solve that fails. An impossible month announces itself. A derived series inverting perfectly looks exactly like validation, and nothing about it appears wrong.

What to doThe solver flags a year whose stored series is marked derived. Before treating any agreement as independent, check whether the two things you are comparing share an origin. A second opinion has to come from a different input, not a different direction through the same one.

Repeating a claim you did not check

Findings arrive secondhand, from another analyst, another session, an old note. Repeating one without testing it spreads a wrong constraint faster than the original error ever would.

What happenedTwo claims about the parish data were passed along and stated as fact: that reloading the source would destroy the prepaid and net-export split, and that parish coverage stopped in April. Both were wrong. The source is account-level detail with no bucket column at all. The buckets are derived during aggregation, so re-aggregating reproduces every one of them and adds parish. And the monthly files run to July with no gap. Checking took two minutes; the claims had already been repeated into a plan.

Assuming a button did something

A guard clause inverted the wrong way made Commit as Budget return immediately every time it was pressed. No error, no toast, nothing written, for as long as the feature had existed.

What happenedThe budget-lock pipeline was built, shipped and believed to be working. The database showed the last write predated it by two days. Confirm a write landed; do not infer it from the absence of an error.

Every assumption carries a basis, and it matters more than the number. rate means someone set the rate and accepted whatever outcome fell out. volume_target means it was back-solved to hit a stated volume, with basis_target recording what that volume was. Without it the value is not reproducible, because a figure like 4.664339 means nothing on its own. carried means it came from measured history and nobody chose it; labelling those as rate would imply a judgement that was never made.

A solved value read as an authored one is the single most common way this forecast has been misread. Check the basis before you change a number.

A solved series carries a second field, method, because basis and method answer different questions. Basis says it was back-solved; method says which solve: exact, smoothed or derived. Two of those take the rolling targets as given and one does not, and nothing in the values themselves tells them apart.

When more than one person is working Agree who owns the assumption tables before starting, and announce every write to them. A solved account adjustment looks arbitrary on its face. One shutdown runs from −50% to +65% across nine months and is only coherent as a solve. Editing those values without knowing they were solved breaks the account.
