Sales Analysis — Attribution Check & Requirements Review

Sales Forecasting & Analysis · YTD January–August 2026 vs 2025 · Prepared against the Sales Analysis Requirements brief · September 2026
This works through the Sales Analysis Requirements brief in the order it was written: the attribution check first, since that was the specific claim flagged for validation, then each level of analysis it asks for, then the full list of questions to consider, answered one by one rather than folded into prose where they'd be easy to skim past. Everything below comes from live billing data and the account–industry mapping, and every number that could be cross-checked against a second, independent cut was.

Attribution Check

The brief poses a specific claim: customer connections are increasing while electricity sales are declining, which it reads as a decoupling of customer growth from revenue growth and a structural shift in demand. That's a testable statement, so it's worth starting there before touching anything else. Because "customers" and "sales" mean very different things across the customer base, the check below is split four ways — residential customers billed monthly, prepaid customers, commercial customers, and then the combined system view the brief's claim was actually made about.

This is where nearly all the customer growth in the system-wide numbers actually comes from, and it's also where the "growth" is least real. Of the 10,308 net new homeowner-tariff accounts this year, 7,399 — seventy-two cents of every new dollar of "growth" — are premises that are connected and billing exactly zero. The small-business tariff shows the same thing at a smaller scale, 58% of its growth. The most plausible read, given the timing, is Hurricane Melissa: homes and small businesses that were damaged or vacated in November 2025 and are still on the books but haven't been reactivated. That's an inference from the timing, not something confirmed directly in the billing data, but it's the only explanation that's consistent with everything else this analysis turned up. Volume tells a more encouraging in-month story than the year-to-date figure suggests — August itself is up about 4.5% year over year, and customers are migrating into higher consumption bands over this period, not lower. The year-to-date shortfall is concentrated in the western parishes that took the storm — Westmoreland down 30.5% in volume, St. Elizabeth 21.7%, Hanover 13.4%, Trelawny 12.0%, St. James 10.9% — while the east and centre of the island are flat to slightly up (Section 4). That is a recovery-lag pattern, not a fading trend.

The three segments above add exactly to these combined figures (Appendix, Table A1). Customer counts run about 23,500 higher in both years than earlier cuts of this same total — that's the small-business accounts that are individually metered, which weren't being added into the system-wide headcount before. Including them doesn't change the growth rate (still about +1.6%), since that population is close to flat year over year; it just corrects the base.

Both halves of the brief's claim are literally true — customers are up, sales are down, every month this year bar two. But "structural shift" is doing more work in that sentence than the data supports, and splitting the segments apart shows why.

Nearly all the customer growth is residential, and most of it isn't really customers — it's inactive connections still on the books after Melissa. Nearly all the volume decline is commercial, and it isn't diffuse — it's two accounts — plus a residential shortfall that sits in the storm-hit western parishes. Prepaid, the one segment growing on every measure, is too small yet to move the total. And the one thing that would actually indicate a structural shift — existing customers using less — doesn't show up anywhere: residential volume is up in-month and migrating into higher consumption bands, and commercial volume outside of Alcoa and the hotel sector is holding steady. That's the opposite of what conservation, efficiency gains, or broad self-generation would look like if it were driving this.

Bottom line for how this gets written up: don't frame it as customer growth decoupling from revenue growth. Frame it as sales growth lagging customer growth because of two identifiable, largely temporary or single-account events on the commercial side, sitting on top of a residential customer count that's partly inflated by inactive connections. That's a very different message to leadership than "something structural is happening to demand."

1. Top Level

Overall, sales are down 5.1% year to date and customers are up 1.6%. Revenue held up much better than volume — down only 1.85% — because the average rate paid per kWh rose 3.4% across the period and absorbed most of the volume shortfall. It's worth remembering that the 2025 base itself isn't a clean baseline: it was already running below normal from November onward because of the storm, so part of this year's "decline" is really "hasn't caught back up yet" rather than fresh deterioration on top of a healthy prior year.

1b. Revenue composition — what is actually moving

Revenue is billed in layers that behave very differently: the non-fuel charges (customer charge, energy charge and, for commercial customers, the demand charge on peak kVA) are the part JPS keeps to run the network; fuel and IPP purchased power are largely passed through; taxes (GCT) sit on top. Separating them shows that the headline revenue decline is not one thing.

Commercial non-fuel charges fell 4.6% against a 5.2% fall in volume, and the reason is in the demand line. Demand charges are billed on peak kVA, not on kWh, and a customer's peak doesn't fall in proportion to its total consumption: a plant that runs less often still hits the same peak when it does run. Across the three demand-billed classes (RT40, RT50, RT70) volume is down 6.3% year to date and the energy charge is down 6.3% right alongside it, but the demand charge is down only 4.9% and billed kVA only 5.9%. That gap is why commercial revenue (−1.3%) is so much more resilient than commercial volume (−5.2%).

3. Within Rate Class — Consumption Blocks

This is where the attribution check's real answer lives, so it's worth walking through carefully. RT10 and RT20 are the only two classes with banded consumption data, and both tell the same story: the Under-150 and 150–350 kWh bands are shrinking while every band above 350 kWh is growing. Concretely, for RT10 the two lowest bands lost 6,762 customers combined while the four bands from 350 kWh up gained 7,570 — more customers moved up than moved out of the low end, which means the migration is being topped up by some new connections landing directly in the middle bands too, not just by existing low-usage customers climbing. Either way, the direction is up, not down. The full band tables for every rate class are in the Appendix (Tables A2–A4).

So the falling per-customer average isn't existing customers pulling back — it's the blended average getting diluted by a large number of connections that aren't consuming anything at all. That's a meaningfully different problem to solve than "customers are conserving," and it points toward a data-cleanup and reconnection question (are these premises actually vacant, or just stuck in a billing/meter-read backlog?) rather than a demand-forecasting one.

Solar shows up in two different ways in the data, and they tell different stories. Customers registered on the net-billing tariff are few and barely growing — about 510 on the residential tariff and 313 on the small-business tariff, up roughly 4–5% in a year, with another 65 large commercial accounts that haven't moved. But the number of customers who actually exported more than they drew in August is close to double the registered residential count (966 against 511) and grew five times as fast (+26%). At least 450 residential exporters are therefore not on the registered scheme, or not yet; that's the activity the brief asks about that sits outside registered net billing. Either way it's still small in absolute terms — about 1,100 customers — but the direction is clear and the registered count understates it. One caution: a single month of net export can also come from a meter correction or a billing estimate reversal, so the export count is an upper bound on solar, not a precise measure.

5b. Concentration — how much of this sits with a handful of accounts?

Taking RT50 as the sharpest example, since it has the steepest volume decline of any class: the five accounts that lost the most volume year over year were four hotel properties and Caribbean Cement, while the five biggest gainers — NFE North Holdings, Half Moon Bay, West Meat Packers, Windalco, National Insurance Fund — don't come close to offsetting them in scale. That's the pattern across every class this analysis touched: the movement is a small number of large accounts, not a broad shift across the customer base.

Questions to Consider

How are we performing from a sales perspective? Growing, declining, or flat?
Declining in volume (−5.1% YTD), growing in customer count (+1.6%), close to flat in revenue (−1.85%, cushioned by the rate increase). See Section 1.
What customer class is driving sales and peak-demand changes? What are the drivers? Where are the shortfalls?
RT50 and RT60-ST have the steepest percentage volume declines; RT70 has the steepest customer-count decline, concentrated in one account. See Section 2.
Which customers explain the gains? Do these reflect business growth, new accounts, return to the grid, or billing adjustments?
Mostly new connections in count terms, but a large share of those are zero-consumption (Section 3), so they're not really adding load. The gains that do reflect real business growth are concentrated in a handful of named accounts — Caribbean Cement, Distilleries — and in the upward migration of existing residential customers into higher bands, not broad-based growth across the customer file.
Which customers explain lost sales? What is the cause, and when is demand expected to return, if so?
Two causes cover nearly all of it: Alcoa's self-generation (RT70, permanent, not returning) and the hotel sector's still-incomplete Hurricane Melissa recovery (temporary, closing gradually month over month).
Where a rate class shows net growth or net decline overall, is that outcome driven mainly by new connections and closures rather than by existing customers, and could that be masking an underlying decline (or growth) among the existing customer base?
Yes, and this is the central finding of the whole exercise. Existing RT10 and RT20 customers are genuinely using more, moving into higher consumption bands, but that real growth is invisible in the blended per-customer average because it's being swamped by a wave of connections that use nothing at all. See Section 3.
Within each rate class, does the number of customers gaining vs. losing consumption align with the direction of net load change, or is the result being driven by a small number of accounts with disproportionately large swings?
A small number of accounts. See Section 5b — RT50's losses trace to four hotels and one cement account; RT70's entire swing is one account.
Which industries are growing or declining, and why? Are changes widespread or concentrated in a few customers/classes?
Concentrated. Hotels alone outweighs the next three declining industries combined. See Section 5.
Within industries, how is the composition of demand changing? Does a higher share reflect growth or declines elsewhere?
Mixed, and worth distinguishing case by case: Cement Manufacturing's rising share is real growth (volume and share both up together); Hotels' falling share is a genuine decline, not dilution by faster-growing peers. See Section 5a.
Where is demand loss concentrated? Which Rate Class? Which consumption bucket?
By class, RT50 and RT60-ST. By consumption bucket, RT10's two lowest bands are losing customers even as everyone who remains in the higher bands uses more. By geography, the western parishes — Westmoreland, St. Elizabeth, Hanover, Trelawny and St. James — carry the whole of the residential volume decline. See Sections 3 and 4.
Is charging demand growing through more accounts or greater usage? Which operators are driving the increase?
There is an EV rate class in use, but only in the forecast layer, and only partly cut over from actuals. The Latest-Estimate table carries 26 accounts under the EV tariff (srat_code EV40) for June 2026: 52,188 kWh and J$1.85M across 11 parishes, one or two sites per parish, every one billed to "Jamaica Public Service" or "Jamaica Public Service Co. Ltd" — this is JPS's own charging network, not third-party customers. Checking why it doesn't appear in actuals turned up the actual mechanism: 3 of the 26 accounts do have real billing history, just still coded under their old class rather than EV — one has been running J$20–30k/month under RT40 since 2024 before going quiet after January 2025, another shows real and growing usage under RT20 through 2025 (7,153 kWh in April climbing to 12,833 by November, which reads like a charging site ramping up), and a third is a negligible ~15 kWh/month RT20 connection. The other 23 have no billing history anywhere. So this isn't "no data" — it's a forecast team that has already reclassified known EV sites onto the new tariff for planning purposes, ahead of the metering and billing side actually cutting them over. Worth asking Commercial/Metering directly when that reclassification is expected to hit the live feed.
How quickly is self-generation growing? How does it affect grid purchases, and how much activity sits outside registered net billing? For declining accounts, is the reduction due to lower usage while remaining connected versus full disconnection from the grid?
Solar shows up in two ways in the data. Customers registered on the net-billing tariff number about 890 (roughly 510 residential, 313 small business, 65 large commercial) and are barely growing, up 4–5% in a year. But customers who actually exported more than they drew in August number about 1,070 across the two residential tariffs, close to double the registered residential count, and grew 26% (RT10) and 58% (RT20) year over year — so at least 450 residential exporters sit outside registered net billing, and that group is where the growth is. The forecast expected 1,632 RT10 net-export customers in August against the 966 realized, so residential self-generation is running behind plan rather than ahead of it. Alcoa's exit was categorically different, a full one-month departure from grid draw (9 GWh to 1.2), not a gradual export pattern. Commercial exporters are rare (about 50 small-business accounts and none on RT40/RT50 in August) and commercial net-billing accounts are flat. There is no record that logs self-generation events as they happen beyond the registered tariff, so anything outside it has to be reconstructed after the fact. See Section 3 and Appendix A9.
How are demand changes affecting revenue across rate classes?
Far more gently than the volume numbers suggest — system-wide, −1.85% revenue against −5.1% volume, because the average rate rose 3.4% and absorbed most of the difference. For commercial customers there is a second cushion: demand charges are billed on peak kVA, which doesn't fall in step with kWh, so across RT40, RT50 and RT70 volume and the energy charge are both down 6.3% while the demand charge is down only 4.9%. RT70 revenue is actually up despite selling less power. See Sections 1b and 2.
Which industries now contribute the most revenue? How does an industry or rate-class change compare in scale to the overall system-level change — is it disproportionately responsible relative to its size?
Hotels lost 26.0% of its volume but only 20.1% of its revenue — a favorable rate or mix shift cushioned some of the blow. Water Supply shows the same pattern at a smaller scale. See Section 5.
What is happening within key customer businesses? Which changes are temporary disruptions versus sustained changes in grid demand?
Temporary: the hotel sector's Melissa recovery, which is closing gradually. Sustained: Alcoa's self-generation, which is a permanent reduction in grid draw, not a dip that reverses.
Is average revenue per kWh changing?
Yes, and it's the main reason revenue held up better than volume — the system-wide blended rate rose from J$50.95/kWh to J$52.68/kWh, +3.4%. Every rate class saw its own rate rise over the period; RT70 (+6.7%) and RT40 (+5.2%) moved the most.

Data Gaps Identified This Pass

A few things came up repeatedly enough while working through this that they're worth listing separately rather than burying in the sections above, since they'll limit how far any future version of this analysis can go until they're addressed.

  • Parish by consumption band isn't available for residential. Every residential account carries a parish in the source billing records, and Section 4 now uses that directly. But the residential band feed into the sales platform combines all parishes into one island-wide total, so a band-by-parish cut still needs that feed rebuilt at parish level.
  • Registered net billing isn't flagged in the sales platform. The net-billing tariff (about 890 accounts) is folded into the ordinary residential, small-business and commercial classes on the way in, so it can only be seen by going back to the billing extract, which is where the figures in this report come from. Customers exporting in the month can be seen in the platform, but that is a different and larger group (Section 3, Appendix A9). Carrying a net-billing flag through to the platform would remove the need to reconstruct it.
  • Revenue components are stored only for commercial accounts. Fuel, energy, IPP and customer-charge splits are held in the platform for commercial accounts only. Residential components in Section 1b come straight from the billing extract, and prepaid isn't split by component in any source, so it appears as a single revenue line.
  • GCT is missing for the larger commercial classes in recent months. Tax on RT40, RT50, RT60-ST and RT70 hasn't been loaded for May, July or August 2026 (and RT60-ST in June looks out of line with earlier months), so tax is shown for January to April only.
  • "Other" revenue fell J$1.2 billion year over year. Adjustments and credits dropped from about J$200 million a month on commercial accounts in early 2025 to about J$55 million in 2026. It accounts for over half of the net revenue decline and should be confirmed with Billing as either real or a change in how adjustments are recorded.
  • EV charging (tariff EV40) is modeled in the forecast layer ahead of the actuals cutover. 26 JPS-owned sites are already classified as EV in the June-2026 Latest Estimate; 3 have real billing history, still coded under RT40/RT20. There's no way to track EV growth, public or JPS-owned, until the metering/billing side finishes reclassifying these accounts into the live feed.
  • Fixed during this pass, not just flagged. Every commercial account on RT20, RT40, RT50, RT60-ST and RT70 was being given the same generic "Commercial" usage tag regardless of what it actually consumed — so a business exporting power to the grid, or one billing zero for the month, looked identical in the data to one drawing normal load. That's why export and zero-consumption commercial customers were invisible to the cuts used elsewhere in this report. Corrected across the full history. Real counts, now visible: RT40 carries 71 net-exporting customer-months and 1,287 zero-consumption customer-months historically; RT50 carries 65 and 166; RT20 commercial carries 783 and 47,614 out of 461,828 records. Commercial customers exporting in the month are rare and not trending sharply (RT40 about 1 a month in 2025 to about 2.75 in 2026, RT50 flat at about 2).
  • Self-generation is only tracked through the net-billing tariff. The planning record that looks like it should log self-generation is forward-looking assumptions for 2027 onward. Anything outside the roughly 890 registered accounts — including a large single-account change like Alcoa's — has to be reconstructed after the fact from billing going to zero or negative.
  • About one kWh in eight of commercial volume carries no industry classification (12.6% in 2026), almost all of it small-business accounts, where 56% of volume is untagged. A tagging cleanup targeted at the small-business file would close nearly all of it (Section 5).

A11. Definitions

  • Residential — billed monthly: RT10 homeowner accounts and the residential-type accounts on RT20, billed after use. Prepaid: pay-as-you-go accounts on RT10 and RT20, reported separately. Commercial: RT20 accounts registered as businesses, plus RT40, RT50, RT60-ST and RT70.
  • Customers are billed accounts in the month: residential counts come as totals by band, commercial counts are one per metered account. Sales are billed kWh. Revenue is net billed revenue before GCT, which is shown separately.
  • Non-fuel charges are the customer charge, energy charge and (commercial only) demand charge; fuel and IPP are the pass-through charges; other is what remains between the sum of those charges and net revenue (billing adjustments and credits).
