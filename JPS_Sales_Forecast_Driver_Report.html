4. New Load and Customer Acquisition: The Evidence

Residential growth comes from two separate places and the forecast measures them separately. Premises join the grid, and households already on it cross into higher consumption bands. The first is worth 13.8 GWh in FY2027, the second 81.7. Confusing the two is what makes residential growth look either unexplained or overstated, so each is set out on its own below.

4.1 The rate at which customers join

The measure is premises connected, taken from the billed customer count for the class. RT10, compound monthly growth:

The full eighteen-month span is adopted: 0.1256% a month, 820 premises, +1.52% a year. RT20 residential is measured from April 2025 at 0.2361% a month, 118 premises, +2.87% a year. The March-to-April 2025 boundary carries a reclassification that moves roughly 5,565 premises out of RT20 residential, so any window crossing it reads as a collapse in the customer base that did not happen. The RT20 count covers the residential segment only; commercial RT20 accounts carry no customer count, so no commercial connection rate is claimed.

Premises connected is the right series for this because it reads through the storm. It fell by 3,325 in November 2025 and was already above its October level by December, so the connection rate is legible across the whole period and no month has to be excluded or repaired.

The two ends fall for different reasons. Over-950 losing 3.6% per customer is the signature of bill response and solar self-supply: the heaviest users have the most to save and are the most likely to install photovoltaic. Note that solar self-consumption appears here, not in the solar driver. A customer who installs panels and consumes their own generation without exporting never becomes a net biller; their draw simply falls inside their band. Part of the −12.8 GWh load decline is therefore solar that the solar line does not capture.

5. Residential Demand: Macro, Migration and Load Decline

Underlying residential demand is the largest single line in both bridges, +83.6 GWh in FY2027 and +68.8 in FY2028. It is not one assumption. It is a macro growth rate applied to the residential cohorts, from which three separately measured effects are carved out.

5.1 The macro rate

DriverFY2027 monthlyFY2028 monthlyApplies to
RT10 macro0.303%0.613%RT10 residential cohorts
Residential macro0.263%0.124%RT20 residential cohorts
Commercial macro−0.624%−0.624%Commercial accounts, before industry rates
These are monthly rates against a rolling three-month base, so they compound, and the face value is not the annual effect. RT10 at 0.303% a month does not mean 3.7% a year: the engine applies it to a base that already contains the prior months' growth, and the residential cohorts also carry seasonality, weather and solar on top. This is the single most common way this forecast has been misread. A 0.676% RT10 macro was once read as 8.1% growth and reported as a 30 GWh error; it was not, it had been solved to deliver a specific volume. Read the class totals in section 1, not the driver rates, for what the forecast carries.

5.2 Bucket migration

Migration is the largest sub-driver at +81.7 GWh in FY2027, and it is worth being precise about what it is and is not. The migration driver rows in the assumptions table are all zero. Nothing is entered as a migration percentage. Migration is what the residential cohort model produces when the macro rate lifts a band and households cross into the band above.

Bands are reassigned every month on that month's consumption, so a household moving from 140 to 400 kWh leaves the under-150 band and joins 350–550. The evidence that this is where the growth comes from, rather than customers using more inside their band, is section 4.4: the under-150 band lost 9,790 customers while the bands above it gained 9,948, and consumption per customer within the middle bands is flat to +0.1%.

Because migration is derived rather than entered, it cannot be tuned directly. It moves when the macro rate moves, and it absorbs the residual between the modelled residential total and the three measured sub-drivers. That is why it is the line that changes when acquisition or load decline is re-measured, as happened when acquisition moved from 12.0 to 13.8 GWh and migration fell 83.5 to 81.7 with no change to the company total.

5.3 Load decline

Set out in section 4.4. Consumption per customer falls at the two ends of the book and nowhere else: under-150 from 76.5 to 73.4 kWh a month, over-950 from 1,799 to 1,735, with the four middle bands flat across the same seven months. Attributed to residential energy conservation and the impact of tariff increases. Worth −12.8 GWh in FY2027 and −11.7 in FY2028.

5.4 Prepaid

Prepaid is carried at 2.9% volume growth against 10.9% observed over the year to July 2026, when the prepaid customer count rose from 15,975 to 17,465 and consumption per prepaid customer rose 1.4% to 163.7 kWh a month. Worth +0.9 GWh in FY2027 and +0.7 in FY2028.

This is deliberately conservative and it is a judgement, not a measurement. Holding prepaid to 2.9% when it is running at 10.9% leaves roughly 2 GWh of FY2027 on the table. The reason is that prepaid growth to date is substantially conversion of existing postpaid customers rather than new load, so booking the observed rate would double-count against bucket migration. If the conversion programme is expected to accelerate, this is the line to revisit.

10. Storm Recovery in the Commercial Book

Hotels across all rate classes, 209 accounts, move from 193.8 GWh in FY2026 to 220.0 in FY2027 and 222.7 in FY2028. The 26.2 GWh step in FY2027 is the recovery. FY2028 adds only 2.7, and none of it is further recovery: RT50 hotels rise 5.5 on the large commercial adjustment, RT40 hotels fall 2.6 as the second-half 2026 annualisation drops out, and RT70 hotels are flat. The large hotel book is back to a normal level by the end of FY2027, which is why RT50 and RT70 carry no normalisation into FY2028.

Normalisation is applied in FY2027 to RT50 (4.66% a month), RT40 (0.28%) and RT20 (0.53%), and to no other class. FY2028 carries half the FY2027 rate on RT40 (0.141%) and RT20 (0.266%) and nothing on RT50: the large hotel and water-supply accounts are back to normal levels by December 2027 and are named, but the small commercial book is not, and the parish evidence says its gap is still open. RT10 carries none because its storm gap closed inside FY2026: it ran 12.5% and 15.4% below prior year in January and February, crossed above prior year in May, and was 5.0% above by July. Industrial carries none following the 29 August decision to withdraw a class-wide adjustment that was reaching non-storm-affected accounts.

11. Basis of Preparation

  • FY2025 is billed actual. FY2026 is January–July billed actual with August–December forecast, tied to the 3,281,970 MWh submitted on 19 August 2026 through a disclosed 460.8 MWh true-up on the open months. Closed months are untouched.
  • FY2027 and FY2028 are full driver projections. Each account or cohort is projected from a trailing three-month base adjusted for macro, temperature, industry seasonality, solar, storm normalisation and confirmed customer events.
  • Operational adjustments are entered per account with a reason code and justification, and are listed in the Key Account Review.
  • System losses are held at a 27.10% twelve-month rolling rate at each December, matching FY2026. The FY2027/FY2028 targets are derived from a monthly loss path rather than set as a smooth rolling series: seasonality is taken from 2024–2025 billed actuals with the Oct–Dec 2025 hurricane months excluded, and the level solved so each year closes on target. Implied monthly rates run 22.78% to 30.72%, inside the 21.84% to 30.96% observed range. The previous targets, smoothed independently of sales seasonality, implied a 0.57% January 2028 and are superseded.
  • The engine, this report, the Executive Summary and the Sales & Generation workbook carry identical figures at rate-class level.
